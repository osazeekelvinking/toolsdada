How Your Due Date is Calculated: Naegele's Rule

Obstetricians worldwide rely on Naegele's Rule to estimate a baby's date of birth. Devised by German obstetrician Franz Karl Naegele in the 19th century, the rule assumes a standard human gestational period of 280 days (40 weeks) from the first day of the last normal menstrual period (LMP).

Estimated Due Date (EDD) = LMP Date + 280 Days + (Cycle Length − 28 Days)

Because ovulation typically occurs 14 days prior to the start of the next cycle, women with shorter cycles (e.g., 24 days) ovulate earlier, resulting in an earlier delivery date. Conversely, longer cycles (e.g., 34 days) push the estimated date further out.

Why Only 4% of Babies Arrive on Their Due Date

A due date is best understood as the midpoint of a normal delivery bell curve rather than an exact deadline. ACOG (American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists) categorizes deliveries as:

  • Early Term: 37 weeks, 0 days through 38 weeks, 6 days.
  • Full Term: 39 weeks, 0 days through 40 weeks, 6 days.
  • Late Term: 41 weeks, 0 days through 41 weeks, 6 days.
  • Post Term: 42 weeks, 0 days and beyond.

Over 90% of healthy pregnancies result in spontaneous labor between 37 and 42 weeks.

First Trimester Ultrasound Dating

While LMP is the initial dating benchmark, an early ultrasound measuring the embryo's Crown-Rump Length (CRL) between weeks 7 and 13 is the gold standard for clinical precision. If the ultrasound date differs from LMP by more than 5 to 7 days, obstetricians will adjust your official due date accordingly.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does Naegele's rule calculate the estimated due date?

Naegele's rule calculates the Estimated Date of Delivery (EDD) by taking the first day of the last menstrual period (LMP), adding 280 days (40 weeks), and adjusting for menstrual cycle variance from the standard 28-day cycle.

What percentage of babies are actually born on their exact due date?

Only approximately 4% to 5% of babies are born on their precise estimated due date. Most healthy births occur within a normal window spanning from 37 weeks (early term) to 42 weeks (post term).

What is the difference between gestational age and fetal age?

Gestational age is measured from the first day of your last menstrual period (LMP), which is approximately two weeks before conception occurs. Fetal age measures actual developmental age from the time of ovulation/fertilization.

How is an IVF due date calculated?

For IVF with a 3-day embryo transfer, add 263 days to the transfer date. For a 5-day blastocyst transfer, add 261 days to the transfer date. IVF dates are exceptionally precise because the exact fertilization and transfer times are documented.
