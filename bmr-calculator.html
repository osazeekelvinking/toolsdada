What is Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)?

Your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) represents the amount of energy your body burns to sustain essential survival functions at complete rest in a neutral temperature environment. Over 60% to 75% of all calories you burn every day are dictated by your BMR—powering vital organs like your brain, heart, liver, and kidneys.

Comparing the 3 Clinical Formulas

  1. Mifflin-St Jeor Equation (1990): The modern gold standard endorsed by the American Dietetic Association:
    Men: (10 × kg) + (6.25 × cm) − (5 × age) + 5
    Women: (10 × kg) + (6.25 × cm) − (5 × age) − 161
  2. Revised Harris-Benedict (1984): An update to the classic 1919 study that factors in secular trends in height and weight.
  3. Katch-McArdle Formula: If your body fat percentage is known, this formula isolates lean body mass (LBM) directly:
    BMR = 370 + (21.6 × Lean Body Mass in kg)

How to Use Your BMR to Lose or Gain Weight

To determine your real-world daily calorie requirements, multiply your BMR by your physical activity multiplier (known as Total Daily Energy Expenditure, or TDEE). For sustainable fat loss, subtract 500 calories per day from your TDEE, creating a weekly deficit of 3,500 kcal (~1 lb of fat). Never restrict calories below your basal BMR without clinical supervision.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)?

Basal Metabolic Rate is the minimum number of calories your body burns in 24 hours simply staying alive in a resting, post-absorptive state—powering involuntary cellular functions like breathing, circulating blood, cellular repair, and brain activity.

Which BMR formula is the most accurate?

The American Dietetic Association (ADA) confirms the Mifflin-St Jeor equation as the most accurate formula for standard adults. However, if your body fat percentage is precisely known (via DEXA), the Katch-McArdle formula provides superior precision because it calculates metabolic burn based directly on lean muscle mass.

Can eating below my BMR cause metabolic damage?

Regularly eating fewer calories than your BMR can trigger adaptive thermogenesis (metabolic slowdown), where your body down-regulates thyroid output, decreases non-exercise activity, and catabolizes lean muscle tissue to conserve energy.

How does building muscle increase BMR?

Muscle tissue is metabolically active, burning approximately 6 to 7 calories per pound per day at complete rest, compared to only 2 calories per pound of adipose fat tissue. Increasing muscle mass permanently elevates your baseline resting energy expenditure.
